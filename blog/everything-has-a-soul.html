Everything Has a Soul An Agent for Every Object in Simulation

Every simulated object is an agent. Interactions are negotiated once. Classical physics is reserved for contact that actually matters.

Shryas Bhurat and Saket Pradhan · Soul Research · UC Berkeley · University of Michigan · September 2026

The document specifies an ontology, the SOUL reference architecture with its buildable specification, measurements of two real clips, five prototype case studies of a first build, and an evaluation program, fixed here in advance of the data that would test it, in which every thesis has a stated way to fail. The case studies are diagnostic rather than confirmatory. Nothing here establishes behavioral fidelity, a cost advantage, or policy transfer.

Open Isaac Sim, MuJoCo, Unity, or Unreal and import a cube. The workflow has been stable for decades. The cube receives mass, friction, restitution, roughness, luminosity. Those scalars are handed to one global solver that applies uniform physics to every body it steps. Interaction is whatever falls out of the solve. The object contributes parameters. Consequences are derived centrally. Those parameters are not what this paper questions, and they do not go away: whatever governs an object still carries the same mass-like and friction-like quantities an asset always has. The question is architectural only: whether all computation of behavior must happen in one central solver, or whether it can be distributed across many coordinated agents, one per object, each responsible for its own.

That stack is a genuine achievement: decades of contact modeling, integrator design, and GPU engineering. Everything this paper proposes runs on top of that inheritance. Our starting point is not that the design was wrong but that it was shaped by the resources of its time. When it was built, nothing in the loop could understand an object’s nature. What has changed is the arrival of machine intelligence that can read, write, and reason about behavior in natural language, per object and in real time. The classical stack, through no fault of its design, has no slot for it.

Everything has a soul, and therefore everything is a robot.

Under the agentic ontology, a book is not a mesh with a mass. It is an agent that knows how to be a book: rest, open, flutter in wind, fall, thud, settle. A vase knows how flowers ruffle as air speed rises. A sanitizer bottle knows that a press on its cap dispenses a small, slightly variable volume with a pump click. A lamp knows its switch. None of these facts need to be consequences of a global solve. They are behaviors: entries in a charter, enacted on demand, negotiated in context.

Two paradigms

The paper contrasts the uniform-physics paradigm with an agentic one. On the left, passive assets feed a global solver. On the right, objects, fields, and the environment itself are agents that exchange typed messages, with a Director arbitrating thinly.

Three theses

The argument rests on three claims. Each is paired with a named experiment in the evaluation program that can falsify it.

Thesis 01

Behavioral sufficiency

For robot learning, the fidelity target is a distribution over task-relevant behavior, not a petal-exact trajectory. The flowers must ruffle; fine-grained petal paths are not required.

Thesis 02

Language as medium

Speech-act protocols finally have interlocutors that understand. Agents propose, commit, and negotiate interaction semantics in natural language grounded by soul files.

Thesis 03

Societies scale

Solvers grow with every awake body. Societies escape through locality and amortization: negotiate once, then replay from cache as novelty decays.

The primary hypothesis is narrower than the ontology, and it is the one the paper stakes itself on. At matched behavioral quality and matched host resources, object-local negotiation with caching lowers the total cost of authoring and running a behaviorally rich world, relative to a centralized language-assisted compiler drawing on the same template library and the same assets. Total cost is authoring hours plus model spend plus runtime, counted from a cold start, with failed attempts, integration, and debugging included. E2 and E3 are what would test it. A result in which the centralized compiler matches or beats SOUL at equal quality kills the economic case for this architecture while leaving Theses 1 and 2 untouched.

Soul, senses, skills

The ontology’s triad is simple. Every object has a soul (a natural-language charter of identity, personality, repertoire, and limits), senses (a sensorium in its own form), and skills (behaviors over its own degrees of freedom). An object with all three is, functionally, a robot.

Soul

A versioned charter: what the object is, what it can do, and within what physical limits. Authorable in natural language rather than through solver-parameter search.

Senses

Not every agent needs eyes. The bottle reads pressure on its pump. The lamp reads its switch. Delivery is filtered by what the sensorium can receive.

Skills

Named, parameterized stochastic behaviors. Flutter, dispense, toggle, ruffle, thud. Sampled from distributions, compiled after negotiation.

Negotiate once, enact forever

Language is the compiler of the world’s dynamics, not its interpreter at every timestep. Novel interactions are negotiated on a slow cognition plane, then compiled into cheap stochastic processes on a fast enactment plane. As the interaction cache fills, language-model invocations per simulated second decay toward zero.

The one-way mirror

The robot being trained is an agent in the world, but not a member of its backstage. It perceives rendered pixels, depth, proprioception, and contact forces. It never reads souls or messages. The message plane has no counterpart in reality. A policy that conditioned on it would break at deployment. Isolation is architectural.

Backstage

Souls, speech acts, negotiation, Chronicle, Director arbitration. Visible to experimenters and to object agents. Invisible to the learner.

Learning agent

Sees only what a real robot could see. Acts only through actuators. Privileged curriculum guidance, if used, is distilled away before deployment.

What the footage says

The case studies report no validated result. They report traces of a first, partial build. Two objects, the sanitizer bottle and the table lamp, have reference footage: numbers about the world, not about SOUL. Their generated half exists too, one Isaac Sim enactment apiece, phase-matched or state-matched to the footage rather than driven by its stimulus train. The three Franka studies (cabinet, teddy, cloth) have no reference clip, so they compare what was declared and negotiated with what the simulator ran. Every artifact is labeled. measured is a number about the world. authored is a soul, a scenario, an input. generated is a transcript, a plan, a rendered film. The case studies are diagnostic. They expose where the chain from a negotiated commitment to executed physics does not yet close.

Sanitizer bottle

measured Six presses on a 20.2 s clip. Mean stroke 9.3 px, CV 0.16. Gel leaves the nozzle at every depth. generated One negotiated press on Isaac Sim. PRESSDOWN was bound. The dose never fired. Nothing pressed the bottle.

Table lamp

measured A field channel: one state bit consumed by every surface at once. generated Flux, tint, and mass written on Isaac Sim. The comparison is state-matched, not stimulus-matched. Nothing in it is a fidelity result.

What they said to each other

The Chronicle records every speech act in Lamport order. Peach is the object. Lavender is the arm. Grey lines are the runtime. Each episode is one language call that binds one process. The clip sits on the negotiation it produced. Where a bubble names a different site than the prose, that is a disagreement in the record, not an editing artifact.

What the first build showed

The integration failures are the paper’s most reliable empirical content. Of the five objects, zero of the cloth sheet’s six declared material properties and zero of the teddy bear’s seven reached the simulator. Of the cabinet drawer’s three declared routes none was wired, and the one quantity where a declared and an enacted value both exist differs by a factor of five. Every run records zero vetoes and zero escalations: a coverage fact, not a compliance fact, because the constraints were largely unevaluated. In the one case where an agent refused on a constraint, the host opened the gripper anyway, on a fixed phase of the shoot script. A refusal the execution layer can ignore is a recorded intention, not enforcement. Every statement in the architecture about a soul governing physical behavior should be read against that.

The architecture’s contribution is that the number is countable. Every route is declared in a soul. Every unwired route is reported by the plan compiler and the scenario check. Every constant that took a route’s place is in a script under version control.

A single escalation rule

Trajectory-level physics is not retired. It becomes a budgeted resource. Ambient content can run hot: any plausible ruffle is a correct ruffle. Task-critical contact runs cold. Escalation collapses the distribution to a solver island for the duration of criticality. Physics where it matters. Behavior everywhere else. The first build records zero escalations. That is the same coverage fact as the zero vetoes: nothing had the opportunity to fire.

Evaluation program

This paper reports no controlled experiment. The protocol that would run one is fixed here, before the data that would test it exists. Runtime correctness, model validity, and learning validity are three layers. No result in one is spent as evidence in another. All raters blind. All seeds and Chronicles released.

Scope of the claim

We do not claim language-mediated behavior matches solver physics at trajectory level. We do not claim this build is constraint-respecting, or that a soul currently governs physical behavior through the seam the architecture describes. We claim that at behavior level, agentic worlds can be made indistinguishable in distribution for broad classes of everyday interaction; that policies trained in such worlds can transfer no worse where behavioral richness matters; and that the cost of a populated, authored world falls as negotiation amortizes into playback. Each of those claims has a named experiment that can kill it. SOUL is offered as an architectural proposal carrying diagnostic prototype evidence, specified to the point of falsifiability and not yet tested against its own criteria.

Conclusion

The uniform-physics simulator hands every object a mass and a friction coefficient and asks a single solver to think for the whole world. We have argued for its inversion: give every object a soul, senses in its own form, and a repertoire of skills (at which point everything is a robot) and let the world think for itself. Interaction is a closed sensorimotor loop carried over a typed protocol. Language is the compiler of dynamics rather than its interpreter. A thin layer of inviolable physics holds veto over an otherwise negotiated world. The one agent kept innocent of all of it is the robot being trained, which perceives nothing a real robot could not. The flowers must ruffle. Which petal goes where is nobody’s business.

What would stand between this position and a paradigm is execution: a Ruffle Test that meets its pre-registered criteria; an authoring study that times the wall coming down; an amortization run that shows the language bill decaying toward zero; and one robot, trained behind the one-way mirror of a talkative world, that transfers to the real one as well or better at equal lifecycle cost. That is the program. The first build’s job was to leave the seam visible. The next work is to close it, then ask whether the resulting worlds are sufficient for robots that must act with care.

The equations of motion will keep governing the parts of the world that need them. For the rest, we propose giving the world’s things souls, and letting them speak for themselves.
